Truths of Brackvaen

- **Salt does not let go.** Anything laid in the pans long enough keeps: flesh, timber,
  rope, ink, an animal, a man. It does not rot, it does not soften, and it does not
  entirely finish whatever it was doing. A cured thing is not alive and is not inert, and
  the difference between the two is a question Brackvaen answers case by case rather than
  as a rule. Everyone here knows to weight a body before they sink it, and nobody will say
  what happens when the weight comes off.

- **Debt is weighed, not counted, and it survives the debtor.** An obligation here is
  reckoned in salt-weight and recorded on a board, and when a debtor dies the weight
  attaches to whoever takes up their goods - their tools, their boat, their place in a pan
  crew. It is not considered cruel; it is considered arithmetic. The practical effect is
  that a dead stranger's kit is never free, and a party that strips a corpse in Brackvaen
  has bought something along with the boots.

- **Old Rede letters are treated as still in force.** A thing cut in the old tongue on
  stone is obeyed - a boundary, a prohibition, a name - by people who cannot read it and by
  some things that are not people. Nobody defaces one. There is no law against it and no
  stated penalty, and if you ask why not you get an answer about someone's grandfather. The
  Assize's own boundary stones work for exactly this reason, which the Assize would rather
  not examine.

- **Standing water in the pans gives off a sour, heavy air** - the locals call it the
  gorm - which collects in cuts, cellars, and any worked space below the waterline, and it
  worsens through the day and clears in wind. It does not smell like much. It puts out a
  lamp before it puts down a man, and a wise crew carries a bird. This is the reason
  Siwapelu is worked at dawn and abandoned by noon.

- **The Brined are a category of person, not a condition.** Anyone the salt has taken into
  their skin - pan-hands after thirty years, and now anyone who spends long enough below -
  is Brined, and Brackvaen has a settled and entirely practical way of treating them: they
  cannot inherit, because a debt cannot attach to them; they cannot be sworn against,
  because an oath needs something to hold; and they are not turned out, because they work
  and they do not sicken. They are useful, they are excluded, and both facts are older than
  the stair.
