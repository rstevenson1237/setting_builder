## Referee Overview

**Approach:** The footing dries. Coming off the sunk span a party's boots stop making water noise inside three paces and the change is complete. It is the first dry ground in four miles and a party will want to stop on it.

**Terrain:** Two miles a watch on the road, which comes up dry here and stays dry for a mile and a half. Off it, this is the only ground in the region that takes a foot: firm grey ash-ground at one mile a watch, walkable, for about two hundred yards either side.

*Decision:* stop here or push on to the frame. Stopping costs the watch and nothing else, and this is one of two places in the Ashen Fen where that is true. Pushing on arrives at the causeway bend with the light still in hand.

*Table effects:* nothing that works reed or peat can reach a party on the ash ground, because there is neither. The fen-wight row cannot happen here: they stand in shallows and there are none.

*Nil:* no water. Not standing, not running, not gettable, over the whole of it, and a party who did not fill at the last sunk span is dry until the bend.

## Features

### The Ash Ground

Grey, firm, bare, taking a print and holding it through a season, and nothing grows on it (LANGUAGE AND ROOT VOCABULARY, T-LNG-01). The road runs across it on five courses rather than eight, because it did not need the depth (ARCHITECTURE AND TERRAIN, T-ARC-01).

There are prints on it that are not a party's and not a cutter's: barefoot, going inward, in a straight line that does not use the road.

The cold here comes up through the soles on dry ground rather than out of the air, and it is worse where the road runs deepest, which on this stretch is nowhere (HAZARDS, T-HAZ-09).
