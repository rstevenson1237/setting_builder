# Wild - Lore

## Decides
What written or recorded thing a WILD location holds, and how it survived being outdoors.

## Read at
Step 4c, when a spec line draws lore. Criteria are in `patterns/setting/Lore.md`.

## Spec

```
LORE
  1     Physical form, and why weather has not destroyed it
  1     Whose voice, and what they were wrong about
  1     Something it names that the party has not found yet
  30%   Partial - the rest is missing, and where it went is knowable
```

**Outdoors, lore is cut, not written.** Paper, ink and hide do not last in the open, so
WILD lore is in stone, metal, bone, or arrangement - things made to outlast the people who
made them, which is why they were made that way. That is a different register from a
dungeon's ledgers and letters: monumental, public, and meant to be read by strangers.

Record a stub row in `setting/Lore.md` now. Content is written at 4d.

## Patterns

**Forms that survive** - a boundary stone with terms cut into it; a milestone naming
distances to places that no longer exist; a grave marker; a memorial to an event; a
territorial claim; a warning cut at a threshold or a crossing; a maker's mark; a tally
scored into rock; a carved figure that is a record rather than a decoration; something
scratched on metal and buried; a pattern of stones that is a message to whoever can read
it.

**Not written at all** - a cairn built by successive travellers; the way a grave was laid
out; what was buried with somebody; a tree cut in a pattern; an arrangement nobody would
make by accident; a route worn into the ground that goes somewhere that is not there any
more.

**What it does** - names a place; fixes a date against `setting/History.md`; establishes
that this country was claimed, worked, or fought over; contradicts what the party was told
in a settlement; gives the older name of somewhere they know by a newer one; records a
warning that is still good.

**Whose voice** - the people who cut it were making a claim, marking a boundary, or
warning somebody. All three are partial by nature, and all three had reasons to overstate.
A boundary stone is an argument, not a fact.
